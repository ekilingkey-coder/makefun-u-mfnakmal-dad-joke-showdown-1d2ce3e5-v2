## Adversarial judge pass — top 3, house-judge voice

Backpack vs wallet. C1 equal: both are table-register object puns. C2: "back" is the idiom's
exact word and the backpack's exact position, so the pivot costs the reader nothing; wallet
requires the unstated back-pocket association. First difference decides: backpack, margin 2.

Backpack vs elevator. C1 equal. C2: elevator's "lifts my spirits" is clean, but "lift" and
"spirit" are two separate transfers with no shared pivot word; backpack shares one word. C4:
backpack ends on the pivot; elevator ends on "spirits", the idiom's tail. Backpack, margin 2.

Wallet vs elevator. C1 equal. C2: wallet shares the pivot word "back"; elevator spreads the
transfer over two words. Wallet, margin 1.

Clear-margin winner: backpack. Runner-up: wallet.

## Winner and why it beats the runner-up on criteria 2-4

Chosen: "My backpack always has my back."

Criterion 2 — the pivot word "back" does double duty exactly (idiom: supports me; object:
strapped to my back). Wallet needs "back pocket" supplied by the reader, so its pun is one
inference deeper; a head-to-head judge stops at the first criterion that differs and takes backpack.
Criterion 3 — the scene (a parent carrying the school bag) is concrete and current; wallet's
back-pocket setup is a recycled pun template, so freshness also favours backpack.
Criterion 4 — 31 characters, six content words, no filler; the final stressed syllable is the
pivot, and "backpack"/"back" echo each other so the turn lands on the last two words. Wallet has
the same shape but no internal echo, so its last word lands softer.
Criterion 5 — "MY backPACK al-WAYS has my BACK" is a clean da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM a father
can deliver across a table; the repeated /ae/ gives the line its bounce.

confirming the pivot, not the object, is the winner's centre of gravity.
